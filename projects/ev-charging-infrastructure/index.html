EV Charging Infrastructure & Load Management

Electric-vehicle charging should be planned as part of the building’s electrical capacity, protection and operating strategy, not treated as a simple socket addition.

The engineering challenge

EV charging introduces a sustained, high-demand load that may operate for several hours. In buildings with existing residential, commercial or mixed-use demand, adding chargers without checking available capacity can increase maximum demand, cause nuisance tripping or require expensive upstream upgrades. Cable routes, outdoor exposure, user access and future expansion also influence the design.

Engineering approach

The sequence below is a practical framework. Site-specific design must be based on inspection, verified equipment information, calculations and the requirements applicable to the actual installation.

  1. Confirm the vehicle and charger requirements, charging mode, rated input current, connector standard and intended charging duration.
  2. Review the existing supply capacity, transformer or service limitations, main switchgear, distribution board schedules and measured demand where available.
  3. Calculate the additional demand and assess diversity or managed charging only where the control strategy is defined and reliable.
  4. Check cable sizing, voltage drop, installation method, thermal conditions, fault protection, residual-current protection and manufacturer requirements.
  5. Plan physical protection, impact resistance, weather exposure, earthing, isolation, emergency access and clear user instructions.
  6. Where multiple chargers are planned, assess dynamic load management, communications reliability, fail-safe behaviour and a practical route for future expansion.
  7. Commission the installation in accordance with the charger manufacturer’s instructions and applicable electrical installation requirements, then document settings and handover information.

Key engineering decision

The central decision is whether charging is unmanaged or actively load-managed. A load-management system must have a defined measurement point, operating limits, communications behaviour and a safe response if control or communications fail.

Verification and handover

A credible handover should make the design intent and the installed condition traceable. Records should identify the equipment and settings actually installed, the tests completed, any limitations, and the maintenance actions required. Results must be recorded from the real installation rather than inferred from a design example.

Verification checklist

  • Verified available supply capacity and maximum-demand assessment
  • Cable sizing, voltage-drop and protective-device coordination
  • Manufacturer-compliant residual-current and DC fault-current protection
  • Earthing, isolation, environmental and impact protection
  • Functional testing of charging control and load-management behaviour
  • Clear labelling, user guidance and maintenance records

Evidence required for a completed project record

Approved scope, site photographs, equipment schedule, design calculations, test sheets, commissioning records and client publication permission.
